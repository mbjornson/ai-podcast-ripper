# 771: Fixing Fairness in the Workplace, with Lily Zheng — Coaching for Leaders

## Summary
The episode addresses the complex challenge of achieving fairness in the modern workplace, noting that while the conversation around Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion (DEI) is highly politicized, there is broad, underlying agreement on the value of diversity. Lily Zheng, a systemic change consultant and author of *Fixing Fairness*, highlights that the problem is not a lack of belief, but a failure in implementation. She cites research showing that the supermajority of people (82%) believe diversity is beneficial for the country, yet many organizations struggle to translate this positive intention into tangible, positive workplace outcomes.

Zheng argues that most corporate attempts at DEI—such as mandatory workshops, sensitivity training, or cultural events—are ineffective because they treat DEI as an *ideology* to show commitment, rather than a measurable *set of outcomes* to achieve. Instead of focusing on temporary, siloed interventions, the solution must be systemic. The core thesis is that true change comes not from trying to mold individuals, but from designing the organizational environment itself.

## Key Points
- **Supermajority Support for Diversity:** Research shows that 82% of people believe diversity is good for the country, significantly higher than the 55% that people often underestimate their peers' agreement to be.
- **The Failure of Siloed Interventions:** The most popular and well-intentioned DEI approaches (e.g., 90-minute training, speaker events) are often the least effective. Organizations fail because they treat DEI as a cultural performance rather than a functional business objective.
- **The Systemic Approach (Water vs. Clay):** The most powerful lever for change is the environment, not the individual. The analogy is that people are like water, not clay. Instead of trying to mold people into a specific shape, leaders must design the "vase" (the organizational structure/culture) that people naturally fill.
- **The First Step: Describe the Shape:** To begin systemic change, leaders must first audit the organization by asking: What behaviors are incentivized? What is the current culture like? If a random person were dropped into this workplace today, what would they be doing, and why? (This reveals the normalized, routinized behaviors).

## Tools & Resources
- **Book:** *Fixing Fairness: 4 Tenets to Transform Diversity Backlash into Progress for All* by Lily Zheng.
- **Concept:** The "Water vs. Clay" metaphor for systemic change (designing the environment rather than changing the people).

## Quotable
> "People are not like clay, they are like water. Rather than trying to mold all of the people inside of our workplaces... what we can do instead is to create... a vase, a shape of the organization that we want people to be in, and people will fill the contours of that vase."
>
> "We don't take the same approach to solving DEI problems as we do with virtually every other problem that we face in the modern workplace."

## Action Items
- [ ] **Audit Incentives:** Identify the top 3 behaviors currently rewarded or promoted by leadership (e.g., "command and control," "siloed work," "hours logged") and determine if these incentives align with the desired culture of fairness and collaboration.
- [ ] **Define the "Vase":** Articulate the ideal organizational environment (the desired "shape")—e.g., "A vase that encourages radical transparency and cross-functional collaboration"—and use this definition as the guiding principle for all policy changes.
- [ ] **Conduct a "New Hire Simulation":** For the next 6 months, observe what a random, highly skilled new hire would naturally do in the current workplace structure. This reveals the *actual* culture, not the *stated* culture.
- [ ] **Shift from Ideology to Outcomes:** When implementing any change (DEI, sales, efficiency), frame it as a measurable goal with clear accountability metrics, rather than a mandatory training or cultural event.
