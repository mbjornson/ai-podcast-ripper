# 775: How to Motivate Younger Employees, with David Yeager — Coaching for Leaders

## Summary
The episode addresses the perennial challenge faced by leaders: motivating younger employees, often framed by the complaint, "Kids these days." David Yeager, a Professor of Psychology at the University of Texas at Austin and expert on adolescent motivation, challenges the conventional wisdom that younger generations are inherently less motivated or responsible. Yeager argues that much of the perceived "decline" is actually a perception problem, rooted in the older generation's tendency to view the world through the lens of their own successful past.

Yeager posits that instead of viewing generational differences as a moral failing, leaders should recognize that young people are highly attuned to the current economic incentives and opportunities—a skill that allows them to quickly adapt to new labor market realities (e.g., the rise of podcasting). The core professional challenge discussed is the "Mentor's Dilemma": how to provide the necessary critical feedback for growth without demotivating the employee, and how to be demanding without being perceived as uncaring.

The solution Yeager advocates is a specific leadership style: "High Demand, High Support." This approach requires leaders to pair tough, high standards with transparent, excessive support, framing the criticism not as a flaw, but as an investment in the employee's recognized potential.

## Key Points
- **The Generational Complaint is Often Self-Referential:** The complaints about the next generation are frequently a reflection of the older generation's own developmental stage and tendency to idealize their past. Historically, every generation has complained about the one that followed (citing Aristotle, Shakespeare, etc.).
- **Young People are Economically Attuned:** Young employees are not frivolous; they are highly skilled at identifying and capitalizing on new economic incentives and opportunities (e.g., the shift from traditional media to digital content/podcasting). This adaptability is a strength, not a weakness.
- **The Mentor's Dilemma:** The conflict between providing necessary critical feedback (which risks offense and demotivation) and withholding criticism (which risks endorsing mediocrity).
- **The Optimal Style: High Demand, High Support:** The most effective motivational style is not "tough or soft," but a blend of both. This requires leaders to set extremely high standards while simultaneously offering excessive, transparent support and working *with* the employee to meet that potential.
- **The Framing is Everything:** Critical feedback must be accompanied by a clear statement that the toughness comes *because* the leader believes in the employee's potential and is committed to their improvement.

## Tools & Resources
- **Book:** *10 to 25: The Science of Motivating Young People* (David Yeager)
- **Concept:** "Wise Feedback" (A structured method of delivering critical feedback by pairing high standards with explicit support and belief in potential).
- **Institute:** Texas Behavioral Science and Policy Institute (David Yeager's affiliation)

## Action Items
- [ ] **Adopt the High Demand, High Support Framework:** When giving feedback, structure it by first stating the high standard/goal, then identifying the gap, and finally, explicitly stating your belief in their ability to close that gap (e.g., "I know you are capable of X, and I want to work with you to get there.").
- [ ] **Reframe Criticism as Investment:** Stop framing feedback as pointing out flaws. Instead, frame it as a necessary, tough love investment in their future potential.
- [ ] **Analyze Your Own Bias:** When complaining about a "new way" of doing things (whether in business or culture), pause and consider if the complaint is truly about the other person's capability, or if it's about how the current reality deviates from your own successful past.
- [ ] **Read:** *10 to 25: The Science of Motivating Young People* by David Yeager.
