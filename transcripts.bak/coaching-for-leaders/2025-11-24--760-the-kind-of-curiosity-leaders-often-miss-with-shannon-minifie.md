# 760: The Kind of Curiosity Leaders Often Miss, with Shannon Minifie — Coaching for Leaders

## Summary
The episode, featuring Shannon Minifie, CEO of Box of Crayons, dives into the critical, often overlooked aspect of leadership: relational curiosity. Minifie and her team leveraged a partnership with the Harris Poll to analyze modern workplace challenges, revealing that despite abundant resources and awareness of external disruptions (like AI and macroeconomic uncertainty), organizations are hampered by internal failures. The discussion argues that the most significant barriers to productivity are not technical or strategic, but deeply human—specifically, fear, lack of trust, and poor communication skills.

The core thesis is that while leaders are aware of the need for growth and feedback, the ability to deliver and receive *hard* feedback is severely compromised. This inability stems from a lack of foundational relationship and trust between colleagues and leaders. The episode reframes curiosity not just as asking questions, but as the willingness to engage in difficult, high-stakes conversations that strengthen bonds and drive performance.

## Key Points
- **The Cost of Fear:** A staggering 14.5% of the work week is lost to the fear of making mistakes. For a 1,000-person organization, this equates to 6,000 wasted hours and $7.5 million annually, indicating that the primary organizational drain is psychological, not logistical.
- **The Trust Deficit:** The root cause of fear, procrastination, and inability to ask for help is identified as a lack of relationship and trust. People are afraid that giving or receiving difficult feedback will break the connection.
- **The Feedback Paradox:** There is a significant disconnect: leaders report that employees *desire* more feedback and growth pathways (up to 80% of leaders noted this), yet simultaneously report that employees are *unable* to receive hard feedback or listen to disagreement.
- **Relational Curiosity is the Solution:** The ability to give and receive hard feedback is directly correlated with the strength of the relationship. Hard feedback is valuable because it makes the recipient feel "seen" and is only truly received when the giver is trusted to care about the recipient's overall well-being.
- **The Leadership Skills Gap:** Despite the massive increase in leadership development content, a dangerous skills gap persists, particularly in the ability to communicate and coach less experienced employees.

## Action Items
- [ ] **Prioritize Relationship Building:** Before attempting to coach or give feedback, focus on building genuine trust. Treat relationship development as a measurable, critical pre-requisite to performance improvement.
- [ ] **Practice "Hard Feedback" Delivery:** When giving feedback, frame it not as a critique of performance, but as an act of care ("I want you to succeed, and this is what I see"). This shifts the focus from judgment to partnership.
- [ ] **Audit Your Team's Trust Level:** Identify areas where fear or silence is prevalent. Use open-ended, non-judgmental questions to uncover the underlying anxieties that are causing people to withhold information or struggle to ask for help.
- [ ] **Read/Review:** The Box of Crayons/Harris Poll report on "Relational Curiosity" to understand the framework and data points.
