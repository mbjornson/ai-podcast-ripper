# 744: Where Being Selfish is Better, with Jenny Wood — Coaching for Leaders

## Summary
The episode redefines traditional leadership concepts by arguing that strategic self-interest—or being "selfish"—is often the most effective way to lead and advance one's career. The discussion challenges the common cultural narrative that leaders must be perpetually selfless, often leading them to burn out or neglect their own needs. Jenny Wood, a former Google executive who spent 18 years climbing the ranks from entry-level to leadership, provides a framework for viewing self-advocacy not as an act of greed, but as "the courage to stand up for what you want."

Wood's core message is that professional success requires actively championing your own agenda and wins. She argues that people are drawn to those who are visibly successful; therefore, leaders must be strategic in sharing their accomplishments (their "wins") rather than waiting for others to notice them. This shift from passive service to active self-promotion is presented as a critical skill for high-performing professionals navigating corporate environments.

## Key Points
- **Redefining Selfishness:** True professional selfishness does not mean hoarding resources, but means championing your agenda—fighting for yourself just as enthusiastically as you fight for your friends and colleagues. Allies are more likely to support those who are already succeeding ("Show them your success is inevitable").
- **The Power of Visible Wins (Shameless Sharing):** To attract high-value connections or opportunities, you must be "shameless" about sharing your accolades, numbers, and successes. When seeking advice or partnership, providing evidence of your own wins makes it easy for the other party to justify investing their time in you.
- **Identifying "Nap Work":** A crucial distinction is between high-leverage, promotable work (e.g., developing AI strategy, improving CSAT by 12%) and low-value administrative tasks ("nap work"). Nap work includes planning team dinners, organizing logistics for conferences, or being the person who replies all to a non-essential email.
- **The "Say Yes/No" Filter:** To maximize career impact, leaders must be disciplined about saying *yes* only to projects that are high-priority and move the business forward (the "big"). Saying *no* to small, time-consuming tasks protects your energy for strategic growth.

## Tools & Resources
- **Book:** *Wild Courage: Go After What You Want and Get It* by Jenny Wood (Authoritative source on self-advocacy).
- **Concept/Framework:** The "Big vs. Small" Filter (Used to triage professional tasks and focus energy only on high-leverage, promotable work that moves the business).

## Quotable
> "In real life, people help those who are busiest helping themselves. Allies flock to your side only once you're going somewhere. Show them your success is inevitable."
>
> "If they're putting the work on the other party to go out and see whether this person is worth spending time with, then you're already doing yourself a disservice. If you are selfish and shameless... make it easy, make it simple, make it fast, do it for them."

## Action Items
- [ ] **Conduct a "Nap Work" Audit:** Review your current to-do list or weekly calendar and categorize every task: Is it *promotable* (moves the business/career) or is it *nap work* (logistics, non-essential admin)? Systematically delegate or decline all nap work.
- [ ] **Practice Shameless Self-Advocacy:** When networking or seeking mentorship, structure your outreach emails to include 2-3 bullet points detailing your own recent professional wins, metrics, and accomplishments *before* asking for help.
- [ ] **Set Boundaries with "No":** Identify one recurring low-value commitment (e.g., planning non-essential team events) that you typically say yes to. Practice declining this commitment by offering a strategic alternative or suggesting another colleague take ownership.
- [ ] **Develop the "Big" Filter:** Before accepting any new project, ask: "Is this activity directly tied to a major organizational goal (e.g., revenue growth, specific KPI improvement)?" If not, treat it as nap work and push back.
