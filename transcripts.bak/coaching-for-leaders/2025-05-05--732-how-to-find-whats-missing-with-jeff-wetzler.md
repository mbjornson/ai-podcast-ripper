# 732: How to Find What’s Missing, with Jeff Wetzler — Coaching for Leaders

## Summary
The episode focuses on overcoming organizational blind spots by identifying and questioning deeply held assumptions—the things leaders assume are true but may not be. Jeff Wetzler, a cofounder of Transcend and expert in human potential, grounds his discussion in the principle that danger often comes not from unknown facts, but from confidently accepted falsehoods ("what we know for sure that just ain't so"). He illustrates this through a personal anecdote about an Uber ride, where initial bias (triggered by a symbol) nearly caused him to retreat. By forcing himself into conversation with someone whose worldview differed sharply from his own, he was able to humanize the "other side" and significantly nuance his perspective.

Wetzler’s core professional insight is centered on developing intentional curiosity. He introduces the concept of "hidden feedback cues," arguing that vital information often remains unstated because people are either hesitant or believe they have already spoken up. Recognizing these subtle, repeated signals—especially seemingly trivial questions or micro-suggestions—is key to uncovering deeper organizational concerns and improving leadership decision-making.

## Key Points
- **The Danger of Certainty:** The primary risk in leadership is not ignorance, but the overconfidence derived from unchallenged assumptions (e.g., "We are safe," "This strategy will work"). This certainty blinds us to critical missing information.
- **Micro Suggestions as Macro Concerns:** Repeated questions or suggestions about seemingly small, tactical details (timeline, data angles, product readiness) should be treated as potential cues for deeper, unstated concerns. Dismissing these as trivial is a failure of leadership curiosity.
- **The Power of the Initial Question:** When faced with conflict or difference in perspective, the immediate impulse may be to withdraw or dismiss the other party. Instead, initiating a low-stakes conversation ("dipping your toe in the water") can lead to profound shifts in worldview and empathy.
- **Bias Mitigation Requires Intentional Exposure:** Overcoming deeply ingrained biases (whether political, social, or professional) requires actively seeking out environments and people that challenge one's comfort zone, forcing a confrontation with complexity rather than simple labels.

## Tools & Resources
- **Book:** *Ask: Tap Into the Hidden Wisdom of People Around You for Unexpected Breakthroughs in Leadership and Life* by Jeff Wetzler. (Focuses on structured questioning to reveal hidden insights.)
- **Organization:** Transcend (Nationally recognized innovation organization).
- **Framework:** The "Hidden Feedback Cue" analysis—a method for identifying repeated, seemingly minor questions or suggestions within a team or client interaction that mask deeper systemic concerns.

## Action Items
- [ ] **Implement the "Micro Suggestion Audit":** In your next team meeting or client review, actively track any question about a small detail (e.g., timeline, minor data point). Instead of dismissing it as trivial, treat it as a potential indicator of deeper, unstated anxiety or concern that needs to be addressed directly.
- [ ] **Practice Intentional Discomfort:** Seek out conversations with people who hold fundamentally different political, professional, or personal worldviews than your own. Approach these interactions with the goal of *understanding their reality*, not debating it.
- [ ] **Adopt "The Curiosity Pause":** When you feel strongly certain about a conclusion (in business or life), pause and ask: "What are we assuming right now that might be false?" This forces a shift from definitive statements to open inquiry.
