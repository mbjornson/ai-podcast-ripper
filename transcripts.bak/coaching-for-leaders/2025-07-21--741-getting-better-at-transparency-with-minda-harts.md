# 741: Getting Better at Transparency, with Minda Harts — Coaching for Leaders

## Summary
The episode explores the concept of transparency in leadership, arguing that while it is often espoused as a corporate ideal, its practical application is difficult and requires vulnerability. Minda Harts, a bestselling workplace consultant and author, emphasizes that true transparency is not merely dumping all available information (which can resemble gossip), but rather building trust by providing clear, honest, and timely context—even when the news is bad or incomplete.

The discussion highlights that effective leadership transparency means acknowledging what *is* unknown. Leaders must be willing to admit they don't have all the answers ("I don't have all the answers, but here's what's going on") rather than pretending nothing is wrong. This approach helps ease employee anxiety and maintains trust during periods of corporate instability or change (e.g., economic shifts, reorgs).

Ultimately, the conversation frames transparency as a two-way street built on reciprocity. If organizations expect employees to be transparent about deadlines, PTO, or performance gaps, leaders must model that same level of openness. In the context of AI and technological leveling, human trust elements are becoming the most critical competitive differentiator for businesses.

## Key Points
- **Transparency is not synonymous with gossip:** The core definition of transparency is "building trust and reducing ambiguity by providing clear, honest and timely information." It is a structured act of communication, not an uncontrolled release of all data.
- **The Power of the Blank Spot:** Being transparent about *what information is missing* or what decisions have not yet been made is often more helpful than generic statements. This manages expectations and prevents employees from filling the void with speculation.
- **Reciprocity in Trust:** Trust is a two-way street. If leaders demand transparency from direct reports (e.g., needing to know when deadlines will be missed), they must provide that same level of openness themselves. Honesty equals honesty; transparency begets transparency.
- **Vulnerability as Leadership Skill:** Effective leadership requires the vulnerability to admit uncertainty. A leader who says, "I don't have all the answers, but here's what's going on," is more trusted than one who maintains a false sense of control.
- **The Future Differentiator:** As AI levels the playing field by automating many data entry and routine tasks, the human element—specifically trust and transparent communication—will become the most valuable and difficult resource for organizations to compete on.

## Tools & Resources
- **Book:** *Talk to Me Nice: The Seven Trust Languages for a Better Workplace* (Minda Harts)
- **Concept/Framework:** The "Two-Way Street" of Trust, emphasizing that organizational expectations for employee transparency must be matched by managerial transparency.
- **Skill Focus:** Developing the ability to communicate uncertainty and ambiguity clearly ("I can't provide this information right now, but I need you to trust in me...").

## Action Items
- [ ] **Skill Development:** Practice communicating uncertainty in professional settings. When faced with a question you cannot answer, use the phrase: "I don't have all the answers right now, but here is what I do know, and I will follow up by X date."
- [ ] **Career Move:** Audit your current team communication flow. Identify areas where information gaps exist (e.g., budget changes, strategic pivots) and proactively communicate not just *what* is happening, but also *what the decision timeline looks like*.
- [ ] **Mindset Practice:** When receiving feedback or needing clarity from a direct report, immediately reflect on whether you are demanding transparency while withholding context or information yourself. Ensure your own communication models the openness you expect.
- [ ] **Reading/Listening:** Check out Minda Harts' work to deepen understanding of trust languages in the workplace.
