# 786: The Problem with Reorgs and How to Do Better, with Phil Le-Brun — Coaching for Leaders

## Summary
The episode addresses the common failure points of organizational restructuring (reorgs), arguing that most attempts are fundamentally flawed because they focus on changing visible artifacts—like the org chart—rather than addressing the underlying behavioral systems and social fabric. The guest, Phil Le-Brun (an executive at Amazon Web Services and former corporate VP/CEO at McDonald's), introduces a powerful metaphor: comparing rigid, predictable organizations to mechanical "Tin Men" versus adaptable, decentralized organisms like the "Octopus." He argues that modern work requires the agility of the octopus—where shared vision guides independent, curious actions.

Le-Brun emphasizes that traditional reorgs fail because they disrupt organic connections and force employees into a defensive "fight or flight" mode. Instead of massive structural overhauls, he advocates for creating psychological safety and fostering adaptability at the team level. The core solution presented is shifting focus from *where* work happens (the silo) to *how* it gets done (the collaborative process), utilizing small, highly focused teams that are given agency to solve defined business outcomes.

## Key Points
- **The Org Chart Fallacy:** An organizational chart is merely an "artificial construct" and does not reflect how work actually happens. Work naturally occurs horizontally across departments (e.g., IT calling Marketing) and cannot be contained within neat, siloed boxes.
- **The Octopus Model for Modern Business:** Organizations should emulate the octopus: having a shared central vision ("shared clarity through a central brain") while allowing individual "arms" (teams/employees) to act independently, learn quickly, and adapt their methods (even reprogramming RNA) based on environmental needs.
- **Behavior Over Structure:** The most effective way to improve an organization is by changing the *mental models* and *behaviors* of its people, not by rewriting reporting lines or departments. Attempting a massive reorg without understanding these complex organic connections is counterproductive.
- **The Two Pizza Team Principle (Dunbar's Number Application):** To maximize trust-based relationships and minimize communication failure points, teams should be kept small—no larger than the number of people who can be fed with two pizzas (typically 8–12 people). These dedicated units must have a clear business outcome ("the why" and "what") but are given maximum agency to determine *how* they achieve it.
- **The Danger of Disbanding Teams:** High-performing teams require sustained focus, time for development (forming, storming, norming), and nurturing. The worst form of corporate vandalism is disbanding these successful units before their work is complete.

## Tools & Resources
- **Book:** *The Octopus Organization: A Guide to Thriving in a World of Continuous Transformation* (Co-authored by Phil Le-Brun and Jana Werner).
- **Framework:** Two Pizza Teams (A model for structuring small, highly autonomous teams focused on achieving specific business outcomes).
- **Concept:** Dunbar's Number (Used here to illustrate the limit of trust-based relationships, advocating for smaller team sizes to maintain high communication efficiency).

## Action Items
- [ ] **Audit Your Org Chart:** Identify key cross-functional processes in your current role or department. Map these connections to prove they operate *outside* of the formal reporting structure (this highlights where true value is created).
- [ ] **Implement "Two Pizza" Pods:** If leading a project, restructure it into small, dedicated teams (8–12 people) with clear, measurable business outcomes defined at the outset.
- [ ] **Focus on Agency:** When defining team goals, ensure that the *how* is left open to the team's collective decision-making, rather than imposing detailed processes and governance from above.
- [ ] **Read/Study:** *The Octopus Organization: A Guide to Thriving in a World of Continuous Transformation*.
