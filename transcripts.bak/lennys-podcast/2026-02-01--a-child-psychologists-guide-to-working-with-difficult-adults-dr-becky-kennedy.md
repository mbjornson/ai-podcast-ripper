# A child psychologist’s guide to working with difficult adults | Dr. Becky Kennedy — Lenny's Podcast

## Summary
Dr. Becky Kennedy, a clinical psychologist and founder of the Good Inside movement, argues that the fundamental principles governing healthy relationships—whether with children, partners, or colleagues—are universal. The core thesis is that many workplace conflicts and poor professional behaviors stem not from malice or incompetence, but from unmet fundamental human needs. She posits that viewing adults through a "parenting lens" allows leaders to approach conflict with greater empathy and strategic effectiveness.

The episode introduces several powerful psychological frameworks for leadership, including the necessity of separating a person's behavior from their identity, and prioritizing "resilience over happiness." Instead of aiming for perfect professional outcomes, the goal should be building a culture where people feel safe enough to fail, learn, and engage in the difficult work of repairing trust after conflict.

## Key Points
- **Separate Behavior from Identity:** The quickest way to derail a professional conversation is to assume a person's character based on a single action (e.g., "They are lazy because they were late"). The goal is to address the *behavior* ("You were late") without assigning a negative *identity* ("You are irresponsible").
- **The Power of Repair:** Repair is the single most critical relationship strategy. It is the willingness to take responsibility for your part in a conflict, acknowledge the impact of your actions on the other person, and commit to doing things differently next time. This process is what builds secure attachment (trust) in any relationship.
- **Connecting Before Correcting:** When addressing an issue, the priority must be establishing connection and empathy first. Only after the other person feels heard and understood should you introduce the correction or feedback. This prevents the conversation from becoming a defensive battle.
- **Boundaries vs. Requests:** A critical distinction for professional communication:
    *   **Request:** Requires the other person to *do* something for you.
    *   **Boundary:** Is a statement about what *you* will do, regardless of what the other person does. (Example: "I will not be able to take calls after 6 PM," vs. "Can you call me before 6 PM?").
- **Resilience Over Happiness:** A successful work culture should not aim for constant, superficial happiness. Instead, it should foster resilience—the ability for people to acknowledge that things are hard and still commit to doing the difficult work.

## Action Items
- [ ] **Implement the "Repair Script":** When you mess up with a colleague, use this structure: 1) Acknowledge the impact ("I realize my tone in the meeting was harsh."), 2) Take responsibility ("That was my fault."), and 3) Commit to change ("I will work on pausing before I respond when I feel stressed.").
- [ ] **Practice Boundary Setting:** Identify one area where you currently make a *request* of others (e.g., "Can you finish this by Friday?") and rephrase it as a *boundary* (e.g., "I will not be able to review anything after 5 PM, so please submit it by 4 PM").
- [ ] **Lead with Connection:** Before giving any critical feedback, pause and ask a question or make a statement that validates the person's effort or emotional state (e.g., "I know you put a lot of hours into this, and I appreciate that. Now, let's talk about X...").
- [ ] **Adopt the "Behavior vs. Identity" Filter:** When reacting to poor performance, train yourself to only critique the observable action, not the person's inherent character.
