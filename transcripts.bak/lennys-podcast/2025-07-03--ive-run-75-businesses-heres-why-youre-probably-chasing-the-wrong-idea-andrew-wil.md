# I’ve run 75+ businesses. Here’s why you’re probably chasing the wrong idea. | Andrew Wilkinson (co‑founder of Tiny) — Lenny's Podcast

## Summary
Andrew Wilkinson is the co-founder and CEO of Tiny, a significant holding company often described as the "Berkshire Hathaway of the internet," which owns over 40 diverse businesses ranging from Dribbble to AeroPress. His background involves having been involved in over 75 different projects or businesses, giving him an extremely broad view of market mechanics. The episode centers on his advice for entrepreneurs struggling with how to generate a viable startup idea and avoid common pitfalls. Wilkinson argues that the biggest mistake is chasing ideas that are universally desired (like cafes) but highly saturated, or attempting massive, complex ventures (like "the next great AI company") before building foundational skills.

His core philosophy emphasizes finding underserved niches—areas of low competition where real money can be made. He advises starting small and simple to build momentum and capital, rather than overcommitting to a high-risk, highly regulated market. Furthermore, he draws a critical distinction between merely running a profitable "job" (which is often miserable) and building a truly scalable "business," which allows the founder to eventually step away from the day-to-day labor.

## Key Points
- **The Niche Advantage:** The most crucial element in business strategy is finding niches where competition is low. Wilkinson cites Charlie Munger's advice: "Fish where the fish are." High competition inevitably leads to lower margins, forcing prices down and making the market unsustainable.
- **Start with Baby Weights (Avoid Overcommitment):** Do not attempt a massive, complex venture (like launching a new bank or building a cutting-edge AI company) as your first business. This is compared to trying to deadlift 300 pounds on day one at the gym. Start with simple, low-barrier services that allow for an immediate "initial win" and build confidence/capital before scaling up.
- **The Job vs. Business Distinction:** A major pitfall is creating a business that becomes a job you hate. If a venture (like a cafe or dry cleaner) does not reach a sufficient scale, the founder will be stuck performing the manual labor of the operation for life. True businesses must have structural scalability so the founder can eventually focus on high-level strategy (sales, marketing, management) rather than physical tasks.
- **The Power of Initial Wins:** The first business should be simple and easy to execute (e.g., a web design agency). This initial success provides immediate positive feedback, builds the entrepreneur's confidence/narrative ("I can do this"), and generates the necessary capital to fund subsequent, riskier ventures.

## Action Items
- [ ] **Conduct Niche Market Analysis:** Instead of brainstorming broad, high-demand ideas (e.g., "a better social media app"), identify highly specific, underserved micro-markets where competition is minimal and pain points are acute.
- [ ] **Prioritize Scalability Over Immediate Revenue:** When evaluating a business idea, ask: "If this succeeds, what part of the operation can I delegate or automate so that I am not performing the physical labor for the rest of my life?"
- [ ] **Start with a Low-Barrier Service (The Initial Win):** Launch your first venture as a simple service (e.g., consulting, basic web development) to generate quick cash flow and build confidence before attempting complex product builds or massive market entries.
- [ ] **Implement Customer Intelligence:** Explore tools like Interpret or Sauce if you are in a product/CX role, focusing on unifying unstructured customer feedback into actionable data points connected to revenue metrics.
