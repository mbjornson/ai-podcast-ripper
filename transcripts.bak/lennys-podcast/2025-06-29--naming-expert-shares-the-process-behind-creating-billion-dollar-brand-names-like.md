# Naming expert shares the process behind creating billion-dollar brand names like Azure, Vercel, Windsurf, Sonos, Blackberry, and Impossible Burger | David Placek (Lexicon Branding) — Lenny's Podcast

## Summary
David Placek, founder of Lexicon Branding, is a pioneer in the field of brand naming, having invented several iconic names including PowerBook, Pentium, BlackBerry, Swiffer, Impossible Burger, Azure, Vercel, and Windsurf. His expertise centers on the fact that a company's name is arguably its most enduring asset—more permanent than product design or messaging. The core discussion challenges the common belief that founders will instinctively "know" their perfect name when they see it; in reality, finding a great name requires deliberate effort and often involves fighting against internal resistance.

Placek argues that successful naming strategies must intentionally push clients toward being *uncomfortable* with the word choice. He posits that human nature gravitates towards comfort and familiarity, which can lead companies to settle for safe, descriptive names (e.g., "Cloud Services X"). The true breakthrough comes when the team is forced to think bigger, bolder, and more imaginatively—a process he calls finding "polarization" or tension within the group.

## Key Points
- **The Power of Discomfort:** Great brand names are often rejected initially because they lack immediate emotional comfort or familiarity. The goal of naming isn't to create something safe; it is to give clients the confidence to embrace being bolder and bigger, arguing that "there is no power in comfort" in the marketplace.
- **Behavioral Gap Analysis over Positioning:** Traditional branding advice (focusing on mission statements, values, or positioning) is outdated for the complex digital world. Instead, effective naming conversations must start by asking: "How do you behave now?" and "How do you want to behave in the future?" This behavioral shift creates a more powerful narrative than static corporate descriptors.
- **The Importance of Conflict (Polarization):** The process requires internal tension. If a team is comfortable with a name, it's likely not the right one. David emphasizes that arguing about the name—and pushing back against initial rejections—is necessary to achieve breakthrough thinking.
- **Case Study: Sonos:** The initial rejection of Sonos was because it wasn't "entertainment-like." The successful pivot occurred when the founders shifted their focus from what *they* wanted (internal comfort) to what the *marketplace and customers* needed, allowing the name’s inherent qualities (like its palindromic structure) to guide them.
- **Case Study: Azure:** Microsoft was initially limited by thinking of names that explicitly included "cloud." David guided them away from this descriptive trap, helping them find a more evocative word ("Azure," meaning blue), which allowed Microsoft to emerge as an industry leader rather than just another cloud provider.

## Action Items
- [ ] **Conduct a Behavioral Gap Analysis:** When defining product or company identity, shift focus from "What are our values?" (static) to "How does our ideal customer behave today vs. how must they behave in the future?" (dynamic).
- [ ] **Embrace Naming Conflict:** During internal brainstorming sessions for branding, actively seek out and argue against initial ideas. View disagreement or resistance as a sign of potential strength in the name.
- [ ] **Challenge Descriptive Thinking:** If your product/service is highly technical or falls into an over-saturated category (e.g., "AI tool," "cloud service"), force yourself to find evocative, non-literal language that suggests leadership and future vision rather than simply describing function.
- [ ] **Study Palindromes/Wordplay:** Pay attention to the structural qualities of words (like palindromes or reversible elements) as potential sources of memorable and unique brand identity.
