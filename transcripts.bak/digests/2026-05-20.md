### The design process is dead. Here’s what’s replacing it. | Jenny Wen (head of design at Claude)

**Summary**

The core argument of the episode is that the traditional, rigid design process—which treats research, discovery, and iterative mocking as gospel—is obsolete, particularly due to the exponential acceleration of engineering capabilities powered by AI. Jenny Wen, Head of Design at Claude, argues that the speed and power of modern tools (like "seven clods" and advanced LLMs) have fundamentally shifted the design role. The focus is moving away from creating beautiful, theoretical mocks and towards guiding execution and establishing clear, short-term strategic direction.

The design field is becoming stratified into two distinct roles: 1) Supporting implementation and execution (helping engineers ship features quickly), and 2) Defining the high-level vision. Because AI models are non-deterministic and change so rapidly, long-term (10-year) visions are impractical. Instead, design must focus on creating highly focused, 3-6 month prototypes that point teams toward a unified, efficient goal, preventing random feature bloat.

**Key Points**

- **The "Design Process" is Dead:** The traditional methodology of "research $\rightarrow$ diverge $\rightarrow$ converge" is no longer reliable. The rapid advancements in engineering tooling (AI, microservices, APIs) are forcing the design role to adapt, not the other way around.
- **Mocking is Decreasing:** The proportion of time spent on creating beautiful mocks and prototypes has dropped significantly, moving from an estimated 60-70% to 30-40%. Designers must now be comfortable letting engineers "cook" and prioritizing execution over polish.
- **Vision Scope Shrinks:** Long-term (5-10 year) product visions are replaced by highly focused, 3-6 month directional prototypes. The goal is no longer to create a beautiful deck, but to provide enough direction to ensure that rapid, decentralized feature building remains aligned toward a single, greater cause.
- **The Value Shift: From Theory to Execution:** The most successful path to a product is not through extensive research and theoretical iteration, but through the ability to *execute*, test with *real data*, and gather feedback from *real users* (Build $\rightarrow$ Iterate $\rightarrow$ Test). This is critical when dealing with non-deterministic AI models.
- **The Designer's New Role:** Designers are increasingly expected to be hands-on implementers, focusing on "last-mile polish" and working closely with engineers to bring features across the finish line, rather than just providing static mockups.

**Action Items**

- [ ] **Shift Focus from Mocking to Direction:** When starting a new project, immediately define a concrete, 3-6 month directional prototype/goal rather than spending time building a comprehensive 5-year vision deck.
- [ ] **Embrace the "Builder" Mindset:** Actively seek opportunities to move from pure conceptual design to "last-mile polish" and implementation. Learn to work with engineers to prototype features in actual code, not just in Figma.
- [ ] **Prioritize Execution over Theory:** When evaluating product ideas, favor the path that involves rapid building, real user testing, and data collection over the path that requires extensive, theoretical research and mockups.
- [ ] **Study Orchestration:** Investigate how modern systems are built using microservices and APIs (like the concepts behind Orcus) to better understand the technical constraints and possibilities that dictate product design.

**Key Points**

- **Intelligent Friction is Essential:** In financial services, friction (like verification steps or security checks) is not merely a hurdle to be removed, but a necessary guardrail. Eliminating all friction simultaneously increases the attack surface for fraudsters.

- [ ] **Build Community into Security:** If your product deals with a sensitive or negative topic (like fraud, debt, or poor health), frame the solution not just as a tool, but as a collective service that allows users to feel like they are "part of the solution" with their peers.

### Episode 252: Understanding Product vs Project Management

**Summary**

The episode tackles the common misunderstanding and "battle" between Product Management (PM) and Project Management (PjM), concluding that they are not competing roles but rather complementary disciplines essential for organizational success. The discussion emphasizes that great product delivery requires not just skilled PMs, but robust systems, processes, and organizational cultures that support value creation.

The core distinction is defined by focus: PM is concerned with the *Why*—the problem being solved, the value created, and the optimal solution (discovery work). PjM is concerned with the *When* and *How*—coordinating resources, managing timelines, and ensuring execution. The speaker notes that while Agile methodologies initially aimed to remove the dedicated Project Manager role, the responsibility often fell to PMs, leading to a common misunderstanding that PMs are simply glorified project coordinators.

Ultimately, while PMs own the roadmap and the priority (the "why"), dedicated Program Managers or PjMs are invaluable when dealing with large, complex initiatives that have multiple dependencies and cut across various product lines. For smaller companies, the roles are often absorbed by PMs and engineering leads, but the strategic difference between defining the problem and executing the solution remains critical.

**Key Points**

- **PM vs. PjM: Complementary, Not Competitive:** The roles are distinct skill sets. PMs focus on *value creation* and *problem definition* (the "why"), while PjMs focus on *execution* and *resource coordination* (the "when").
- **The Discovery Gap:** Product Management is primarily about "discovery work"—figuring out what to build, calculating value, and setting success metrics—a function that goes far beyond merely coordinating resources or timelines.
- **The Program Manager Sweet Spot:** Dedicated Project or Program Managers are most valuable in large, complex organizations dealing with initiatives that have multiple dependencies and span across several different product lines or departments. They act as steerer groups for the PMs.
- **The Agile Misconception:** The implementation of Agile frameworks often resulted in PMs absorbing significant project management responsibilities (like creating Gantt charts and tracking dates), blurring the lines and leading to the misconception that PMs are just project managers.
- **Small Company Model:** In smaller, contained environments, PMs, engineering leads, and UX leads often must take on both PM and PjM responsibilities. However, this does not negate the need for the PM to maintain ownership of the strategic "why."

**Action Items**

- [ ] **Develop "Why" Ownership:** Before initiating any new project or feature, force yourself to spend disproportionate time on problem definition, value calculation, and success metric setting (the PM function) rather than jumping straight into resource allocation (the PjM function).
- [ ] **Identify Cross-Functional Gaps:** When working in a large company, identify initiatives that span multiple product lines or departments. Advocate for, or seek out, a Program Manager role to help coordinate the dependencies, allowing you to focus purely on the strategic "why."
- [ ] **Study Investment Strategy:** If interested in high-growth tech, utilize platforms like AngelSquad to learn how to evaluate and invest in startups alongside experienced operators.
- [ ] **Refine Role Boundaries:** When managing a team, clearly delineate who owns the strategic *problem* (PM) versus who owns the logistical *timeline* (PjM/Program Manager) to prevent scope creep and role confusion.

### Enterprise AI Buying Process: Why Roadmaps Fail in the Build Room

**Summary**

The episode analyzes the recent security breach involving McKinsey's AI platform, "Lily," where an autonomous agent was compromised via a simple SQL injection, allowing an attacker to rewrite the AI's core reasoning prompts. While the immediate industry reaction focused on "technical hygiene"—patching endpoints and sanitizing inputs—the speaker argues this is missing the point. The failure was not a technical oversight by the engineers, but a systemic failure in the *process* of building and buying AI software.

The core thesis is that the traditional enterprise software procurement sequence (Strategy $\rightarrow$ Procurement $\rightarrow$ Security $\rightarrow$ IT $\rightarrow$ Development) worked for bounded SaaS applications (like Salesforce or Workday). However, this sequence is fundamentally broken by the nature of AI agents. Agents are "unbounded," capable of crossing multiple, disparate systems (CRM, support tickets, internal wikis) and assembling business context from scratch. This complexity requires a level of auditable, coded permissions and workflow orchestration that cannot be treated as an afterthought.

For any company building or buying AI, the speaker warns that the implementation question is no longer downstream of the strategic decision; it *is* the strategic decision. If the platform cannot guarantee secure, auditable, and cost-effective cross-system access for an agent, the entire roadmap is non-viable.

**Key Points**

- **The Failure is Process, Not Patching:** The root cause of the Lily breach is not a lack of technical skill (McKinsey has great engineers) but a structural assumption that the purchased software could handle the complexity of autonomous, cross-system agentic access.
- **The Broken Procurement Sequence:** The traditional sequence (Strategy $\rightarrow$ Procurement $\rightarrow$ Security $\rightarrow$ IT $\rightarrow$ Dev) is obsolete for AI. For agents, the technical feasibility (can the agent access, read, and write across all necessary systems?) must be defined and tested *before* the strategy is finalized.
- **Agentic Unboundedness:** Unlike human users who rely on the visible screen (the UI is the permissions model), agents operate by querying systems in code.
