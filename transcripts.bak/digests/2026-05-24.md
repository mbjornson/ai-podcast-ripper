### Why most AI products fail: Lessons from 50+ AI deployments at OpenAI, Google, and Amazon

**Summary**

The episode provides deep, battle-tested insights into the unique challenges of building AI products, arguing that the process is fundamentally different from developing traditional software. The discussion, led by experts Kuriti Bottom and Aishwarya Riganti, emphasizes that most failures stem from ignoring these core differences. Their background—leading over 50 AI product deployments across major tech companies like Google, Amazon, and OpenAI—lends significant weight to their advice, positioning them as practical guides for product leaders.

The central thesis is that product builders must shift their focus from merely adding AI features to deconstructing and reconstructing their core business processes. They warn against the "slippery slope" of chasing complex AI solutions and instead advise focusing relentlessly on the underlying user problem. Furthermore, they stress that the AI lifecycle requires a completely new, tighter form of collaboration, breaking down old silos between PMs, engineers, and data scientists.

**Key Points**

- **The Non-Determinism Problem:** Unlike traditional software (e.g., booking a flight, which is predictable), AI products operate on non-deterministic APIs. The user input (via natural language) and the LLM output are both probabilistic and unpredictable, requiring a complete rethinking of the user experience and failure states.
- **The Agency Control Trade-off:** Every time a product hands over decision-making capabilities to an agentic system, the builder relinquishes a degree of control. Successful implementation requires building trust and ensuring the agent's reliability before granting it greater autonomy.
- **Focus on the Problem, Not the Solution:** The biggest mistake is getting caught up in the complexity of the AI solution. The primary goal must always be solving the core user problem, not simply being the first company to deploy an agent.
- **New Operational Model:** The traditional software lifecycle is broken. Success requires a highly collaborative, integrated feedback loop where PMs, engineers, and data specialists work together in the same room, jointly deciding product behavior.
- **Mindset for Leaders:** Leaders must remain hands-on, be comfortable with the fact that their initial intuitions may be wrong, and adopt a mindset of continuous, persistent learning.

**Action Items**

- [ ] **Develop a "Non-Deterministic" Product Mindset:** When designing a feature, assume the user input and the system output will be unpredictable. Design for failure and ambiguity, rather than perfect, linear workflows.
- [ ] **Map the Agency Control Trade-off:** For any proposed AI agent, explicitly define the boundaries of its autonomy. Determine what level of risk/decision-making you are willing to relinquish, and build mechanisms to earn user trust before increasing that agency.
- [ ] **Implement Cross-Functional Pairing:** Break down traditional silos. Schedule mandatory, joint sessions with engineers, data scientists, and product managers to jointly own the feedback loop and define product behavior.
- [ ] **Prioritize Problem Definition:** Before writing a single line of code or selecting an LLM, spend dedicated time validating the core user problem. Use the principles of Strela to gather rapid, continuous user research.
- [ ] **Adopt a "Hands-On" Leadership Stance:** Commit to daily, deep learning sessions (like the example of the Rackspace CEO) where you are willing to admit you don't know the answer and learn from every team member.

**Summary**

This episode serves as a "high-growth handbook" for leaders navigating rapid scale, chaos, and constant change. The guest, Molly Graham, is a highly credible operator who has worked at some of the world's most rapidly scaling companies, including Google, Facebook, and the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative (CZI). Her experience spans the full spectrum of growth: from scaling a communications department at Google (25 to 125 people in nine months) to building a company from nothing ("zero to one") and then scaling it significantly.

Graham's advice is rooted in her personal "scaling battle scars" and is designed to help leaders avoid common mistakes. She focuses on developing frameworks and mindsets—such as the J-curve approach to career growth and the concept of "giving away your Legos"—that allow individuals and organizations to thrive, not just survive, during periods of intense, rapid growth.

**Key Points**

- **The J-Curve vs. Stairs Career Model:** Traditional career progression is likened to climbing stairs (predictable, boring). High-growth, impactful careers are like jumping off cliffs, involving falls, but leading to exponential growth far beyond the predictable path.
- **The "Give Away Your Legos" Principle:** Success requires leaders to proactively let go of skills, processes, or roles that were instrumental in past successes. To take advantage of the next growth phase, you must learn to abandon what worked previously and embrace the next "shiny pile of Legos."
- **The Value of Being "Highly Unqualified":** Graham advises only taking jobs or roles where you are initially highly unqualified. This forces you onto a steep learning curve, which is where the most valuable skills and rapid personal development occur.
- **Growth Stages: Zero-to-One vs. One-to-Two:** There is a distinct difference between building something from nothing (zero to one) and scaling an established entity (one to two). Both require unique toolsets and skills, and understanding this difference is crucial for founders and operators.
- **The Danger of Comfort:** A critical non-obvious take, highlighted by Matt McGinnis, is that feeling comfortable at work is a sign that something is going terribly wrong. Staying in a zone of ease is detrimental to growth and innovation.

**Action Items**

- [ ] **Career Audit:** Identify the "stairs" in your career (predictable promotions) and actively seek out "cliffs" (roles or projects where you are initially highly unqualified) to force exponential learning.
- [ ] **Skill Decommissioning:** Implement a quarterly "Legos review" to identify and intentionally give away skills, processes, or departmental structures that are no longer relevant to the company's next phase of growth.
- [ ] **Seek Discomfort:** When evaluating your current role or company, actively look for areas of friction, ambiguity, or perceived difficulty. If the job feels too easy, it is likely time for a change.
- [ ] **Operationalize Learning:** If you are a founder, differentiate between the operational needs of a "zero-to-one" build (building the initial infrastructure) and the "one-to-two" scale (maintaining velocity and process).
- [ ] **Productivity Stack:** Explore using AI-powered finance platforms like Brex or developer intelligence tools like DX to automate manual, non-core business functions, freeing up time for strategic, high-leverage work.

### Episode 245: Building Product Thinking in Engineering Teams with Matt Watson

**Summary**

The episode defines "Product Thinking" as a critical skill set that must move engineers and technical teams beyond merely writing code (inward thinking) and toward understanding the customer's needs and the business's ultimate purpose (outward thinking). The discussion, featuring Matt Watson (Founder/CEO of FullScale and author of *Product Driven*), emphasizes that great product development is not solely the domain of Product Managers; it requires a systemic shift in organizational culture.

Watson argues that the biggest failure point in tech teams is the lack of psychological safety, which prevents engineers from speaking up, questioning assumptions, or advocating for necessary technical debt. He stresses that the most valuable engineers are those who challenge the status quo and ask "why," rather than simply acting as "order takers" who execute tasks listed on a Jira ticket.

The core framework revolves around establishing a clear, shared *vision*—the fundamental "why" behind the work—and ensuring that all stakeholders (PMs, Engineers, Design) are equally empowered to contribute to the product strategy, rather than having one person acting as a single gatekeeper.

**Key Points**

- **The Shift from Code to Customer:** Product thinking means focusing on the customer's problem and the product's higher-level purpose, not just the technical elegance or completion of the code itself. Nobody cares about your code; they care about what it *does*.
- **The Power of Psychological Safety:** The ability to create a culture where team members feel safe to speak up, push back, and challenge requirements is the fundamental prerequisite for product thinking. Without this, teams become "order takers."
- **Vision vs. Slogan:** True vision is not an HR statement; it is the clear, shared understanding of *why* the team is doing a specific thing that week and *why* it matters to the customer.
- **Shared Ownership of Strategy:** Product Managers should not be the sole decision-makers or gatekeepers. Engineers often possess deep knowledge of the product's legacy, market, and technical limitations that PMs may overlook.
- **Engineering is Trade-Off Management:** Software engineering is fundamentally about navigating trade-offs (e.g., speed vs. stability, performance vs. scope). Recognizing and articulating these trade-offs is a core product thinking skill.
- **The Danger of Inward Thinking:** Many teams get stuck thinking only about the immediate task (the Jira ticket), losing sight of the long-term product goal or the customer benefit.

**Action Items**

- [ ] **Develop the "Why" Habit:** Before starting any task, force yourself (or your team) to articulate the ultimate customer problem being solved, rather than just listing the features to be built.
- [ ] **Practice Product Pushback:** When given a task, don't just accept the requirements. Ask three challenging questions: 1) Who is the customer who benefits most? 2) What assumption are we making about the user? 3) What is the technical debt we are ignoring by building this feature?
- [ ] **Advocate for Psychological Safety:** If you are a leader, actively create space for dissent. Reward the person who asks the most difficult, challenging question, not just the person who delivers the solution.
- [ ] **Read:** *Product Driven* by Matt Watson to formalize the framework.
- [ ] **Skill Focus:** Improve your ability to articulate trade-offs (e.g., "If we prioritize speed, we sacrifice stability; if we prioritize stability, we sacrifice time-to-market").

### AI Project Room: Organize Files Before Asking AI to Write

**Summary**

The episode argues that the current focus on improving AI through better prompts is fundamentally misguided, citing the high-profile failure of a prestigious law firm (Sullivan & Cromwell) that used AI-generated, hallucinated citations in a critical legal filing. The speaker asserts that the failure was not due to the model's inherent limitations, but rather a structural flaw in the working environment—the "data room"—around the model.

The core thesis is that the true breakthrough in agentic workflows is not in the model's ability to write or code, but in its ability to manipulate files. Modern LLMs (like 4.7 and 5.5) can now perform complex, long-running tasks by walking folder trees, inspecting metadata, and comparing dates across documents. The solution, therefore, is to shift the workflow from "Do the thing" (write the memo) to "Build the room" (organize the source material). This process, termed the "Project Room" or "Data Room," establishes a bounded, structured workspace that is structurally antagonistic to hallucinations.

**Key Points**

- **The Failure is Structural, Not Prompt-Based:** The inability to prevent hallucinations is not solved by telling the model "do not hallucinate." The fix requires changing the entire process flow and providing a clean, curated working environment.
- **The Value Shift is to Files:** The most powerful and overlooked primitive capability of modern agents is file manipulation. They can now perform tasks like comparing dates across documents, inspecting metadata, and navigating a folder structure, which is far more valuable than simply processing pasted text.
- **The Project Room Workflow (The 3-Step Process):**
    1. **Initial Prompt:** Do not ask the AI to write the deliverable. Instead, instruct it to *find* and *organize* the source material.
    2. **Data Inventory:** The first critical output must be a "Source Inventory"—a table generated by the agent for every file in the room. This inventory must record the file path, type, date, apparent authority, limitations, and how the file should be used.
    3. **Synthesis:** Only after the data room is built and the inventory is created should the agent be asked to synthesize the final deliverable.
- **Project Room Definition:** This is a bounded, local workspace for a single, serious job. It is more specific than a general knowledge management system and is preferred over shared cloud contexts because it leverages the flexibility and power of the local file system.

**Action Items**

- [ ] **Adopt the "Project Room" Workflow:** For any serious project involving AI, change the first prompt from "Write X" to "Build a Project Room containing all relevant source materials for X."
- [ ] **Prioritize Source Curation:** Before engaging the AI for synthesis, dedicate time to gathering and organizing all source materials (meeting transcripts, old decks, data exports, etc.) into a single, dedicated local folder.
- [ ] **Implement the Source Inventory:** Make the creation of a "Source Inventory" (a structured table detailing file path, date, authority, and limitations) the mandatory first output from the AI agent.
- [ ] **Test Local File Agents:** Experiment with using local file systems (rather than cloud context windows) to run agentic tasks, leveraging the agent's ability to walk folder trees and inspect metadata.
- [ ] **Develop Data Organization Skills:** Treat yourself as the human organizer. Practice finding and consolidating disparate source materials (Slack threads, old PDFs, meeting notes) into a single, usable "data room" before prompting the AI.
