# The Intrapreneurs Rulebook with Chuck House — The Innovation Show

## Summary
The episode centers on "The Intrapreneurs Rulebook," a guide for individuals who drive fundamental change within established organizations. Chuck House, author of *Permission Denied*, establishes a critical distinction: while an entrepreneur builds their own company from scratch, an intrapreneur operates inside a corporation, utilizing the organization's existing resources (funding, marketing, teams). The speaker argues that this segment is often overlooked in leadership training, despite being the reality for most professionals.

The discussion outlines key rules for success within corporate structures. These rules emphasize strategic thinking over mere effort, requiring individuals to focus on making massive contributions rather than small improvements. A major theme is the political necessity of navigating internal power dynamics—specifically, how an intrapreneur must build and maintain relationships with sponsors and bosses to ensure their ideas are funded and implemented.

## Key Points
- **Intrapreneur vs. Entrepreneur:** The core difference is the resource base. Entrepreneurs rely solely on their company; intrapreneurs leverage the corporate structure's entire ecosystem (teams, funding, marketing). This makes the rules for internal influence far more complex than starting from scratch.
- **The 10x Rule of Contribution:** Do not aim for incremental improvement. According to Bill Hewlett’s advice cited by House, a significant contribution must be "10 times better" than what currently exists. If an idea is only 20% better, it's considered minor and easily dismissed.
- **The Danger of Outshining Your Boss:** Drawing from Robert Greene's *48 Laws of Power*, the speaker warns that displaying talent or success in a way that makes superiors feel inadequate (outshining them) is perhaps the worst mistake an intrapreneur can make, as it triggers resentment and envy.
- **The Necessity of Honoring Sponsors/Bosses:** For an intrapreneur, bosses are "lifeblood." Failure to honor those who provide "air cover" or sponsorship—even if they aren't your favorite people—is described as actively undermining your own career (sawing yourself off an eliminatory).
- **Focus on Impact over Effort:** The rules require a clear focus on the desired contribution and its measurable impact, rather than simply spending time debating technical details or past divisional slights.

## Tools & Resources
- ***Permission Denied***: Chuck House's book (and source of the intrapreneurship framework).
- ***The 48 Laws of Power***: Robert Greene’s book, cited for advice on managing power dynamics and avoiding resentment.
- **Kyndryl Institute**: A resource mentioned for continuing education in technology leadership and innovation.

## Quotable
> "If you come up with something that's 10 times better... it gets rid of what was prior."
>
> "Outshining the master is perhaps the worst mistake you can ever make."

## Action Items
- [ ] **Measure Impact:** When proposing a project, quantify its potential benefit using a minimum threshold of "10x improvement" rather than focusing on marginal gains.
- [ ] **Map Sponsors:** Identify 3-5 key leaders in your organization who provide "air cover" or sponsorship, and proactively seek ways to honor their contributions publicly and privately.
- [ ] **Practice Humble Advocacy:** When presenting a bold idea, frame it not as a replacement for current systems, but as an evolution that *builds upon* existing successes, thereby minimizing the perceived threat to your superiors.
- [ ] **Deepen "Why" Questions:** In team meetings or brainstorming sessions, habitually ask "Why?" of both the process and the company's approach to challenge assumptions and drive fundamental change.
