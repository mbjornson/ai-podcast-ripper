# Matt Sellers - What a top 1% design portfolio looks like — Dive Club

## Summary
The episode provides a deep dive into the strategy of creating a "top 1%" design portfolio, moving far beyond generic advice. The core thesis is that a portfolio should not be a comprehensive archive of work, but rather a highly curated *experience* designed to signal specific levels of craft, attention to detail, and intellectual depth. The guest, Matt Sellers, shares his process of intentionally narrowing his body of work, emphasizing that the goal is to make the visitor feel the "level of care" rather than simply reviewing a list of projects.

Matt’s approach is defined by ruthless subtraction. He details how he cut the vast majority of his previous work—initially removing 75% of his projects, and then cutting that remaining selection by half—to showcase only the absolute "cream of the crop." This strategic focus shifts the narrative away from the sheer *volume* of experience (the sum) and toward the *quality* of the signal (the average). The discussion highlights that the portfolio must feel deeply intentional, designed for a specific, niche audience (like experienced design hiring managers) who will appreciate the subtle signals of shared professional experience.

## Key Points
- **The Strategy of Subtraction:** Do not try to communicate the "gravity and scale of the body of your work." Instead, ruthlessly cut. Matt started by cutting 75% of his work, and then cut that remaining selection by half, resulting in showcasing only 12-13% of his total potential portfolio content.
- **Craft Over Process/Metrics:** Consciously de-emphasize process explanations, metrics, and detailed case study write-ups. The primary signal should be the beautiful, thoughtful work itself. The argument is that if the work isn't beautiful, no amount of process explanation will save it.
- **The Danger of Surface Area:** Every extra element added to a portfolio creates "surface area" for a reviewer to poke holes in. The goal is to minimize fluff and maximize pure signal, ensuring that every piece raises the bar rather than lowering the average.
- **Designing for the Niche:** The portfolio must be designed for a specific, ideal reviewer (e.g., an experienced design hiring manager), not for a general audience (like HR or a random recruiter). This specificity allows for highly tailored, inside-baseball micro-copy.
- **The Power of Micro-Copy:** The bio/intro paragraph is critical. It should use shared, specific professional references (like "12,800% zoom") to create a sense of shared experience, signaling that the designer is operating at a deep, specialized level of craft.

## Tools & Resources
- **Paper:** A design tool that uses real CSS, allowing layouts to be designed and then copied as React code, making it a potential future standard for design tools.
- **Jitter:** An AI-powered feature mentioned for design tools that can generate instant motion ideas when a designer drops in their work, making it easier to add a "premium" feel to designs.
- **Bento Grid:** A specific layout structure mentioned as an example of how elements must line up and be intentionally designed for a specific container size.

## Quotable
> "If I can't show you like beautiful, thoughtful work, then no amount of like process explanation is going to save me."
>
> "The importance of thinking about the average of your work rather than the sum of your work."

## Action Items
- [ ] **Audit Your Portfolio:** Immediately identify the 12-13% of your work that represents your absolute best, most polished, and most relevant projects. Be prepared to cut 80%+ of your current body of work.
- [ ] **Shift Focus:** When presenting case studies, lead with the final, beautiful work. Minimize explanatory text, metrics, and process diagrams unless they are absolutely essential to understanding the *craft* involved.
- [ ] **Niche Down:** Define your ideal hiring manager/client. Tailor the entire aesthetic, tone, and content of your portfolio to appeal specifically to that niche, rather than trying to appeal to everyone.
- [ ] **Enhance the Bio:** Rewrite your bio using highly specific, shared industry jargon or cultural references that only an experienced peer would immediately understand. This signals deep expertise and shared professional identity.
- [ ] **Experiment with Tools:** Explore CSS-based design tools like Paper to understand how modern, code-first design workflows can elevate the perceived quality of your work.
