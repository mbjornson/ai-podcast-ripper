# Ryan Scott - The skills that get designers promoted — Dive Club

## Summary
The episode argues that the value proposition for designers must fundamentally shift from demonstrating high craft or visual skill to proving direct, measurable business ROI. In today's volatile job market—characterized by layoffs and rapid technological adoption like AI—traditional design skills are no longer sufficient differentiators; they are merely table stakes. To achieve promotion or become "irreplaceable," a designer must master the language of business impact.

Ryan Scott, drawing on his experience at major tech companies like DoorDash and Airbnb, provides practical lessons on how to think strategically about one's career. He emphasizes that many designers fail because they are selling design solutions (e.g., "I reduced cognitive load") rather than solving core business problems (e.g., "This work will increase conversion by X%"). The key is understanding the entire organizational structure and identifying who holds the ultimate budget control, which is often not the design manager.

Ultimately, the discussion serves as a strategic guide for designers to reposition themselves from creative executors to high-value business partners. This requires adopting an MBA-level perspective on market forces, mastering data storytelling, and framing all work in terms of hard metrics that matter to product leaders and executive stakeholders.

## Key Points
- **The Value Shift:** Designers must move away from selling "delight" or "pretty things" (craft) and instead focus exclusively on the measurable business impact ("hard numbers"). The goal is to demonstrate how design work directly moves key performance indicators (KPIs) or generates revenue.
- **It's a Filtering Problem, Not a Competition Problem:** Job market difficulties are not due to designers being outcompeted by more qualified people. Instead, companies are "over filtering," defining roles with such narrow and specific requirements (e.g., requiring experience managing exactly six direct reports) that they exclude vast numbers of otherwise capable candidates.
- **Identify the Ultimate Approver:** When pitching ideas or oneself for a role, designers often mistakenly focus on convincing the design manager. The true decision-maker who controls the budget is typically the Product Leader or business stakeholder. Messaging must be tailored to this ultimate approver's concerns (i.e., revenue and market strategy).
- **Elevate Data Storytelling:** While metrics are crucial, designers should not limit their perceived value solely to quantifiable data points. They must zoom out and consider other forms of business value that may not have an immediate metric attached (e.g., improving internal process efficiency or reducing organizational friction).
- **Contextualizing Change is Key:** Instead of reacting chaotically to every trend (AI, layoffs, etc.), designers must understand the underlying macroeconomic function causing the change. This strategic understanding allows for proactive career planning rather than reactive "trend chasing."
