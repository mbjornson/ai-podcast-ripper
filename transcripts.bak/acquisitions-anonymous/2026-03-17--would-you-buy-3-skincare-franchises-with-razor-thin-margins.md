# Would You Buy 3 Skincare Franchises with Razor-Thin Margins? — Acquisitions Anonymous

## Summary
The episode is a deep dive into evaluating a specific small business acquisition opportunity: a multi-location skincare franchise in the DMV area. The discussion centers on the financial viability and scalability of the business model, which is presented as a "modern facial shop" that blends spa-quality services with the recurring revenue structure of a fitness studio. The hosts are highly critical, treating the deal less as a sales pitch and more as a case study in due diligence. While the business model—relying on a "triple engine" of revenue—is compelling, the hosts raise significant concerns regarding the valuation, the low net margins, and the inherent limitations of the labor model.

The discussion provides a masterclass in M&A skepticism, emphasizing that the devil is in the details. The hosts repeatedly challenge the provided financials, noting discrepancies between the stated 35% blended margins and the perceived low net profitability. The overall signal is that while the concept has merit (especially the consultative nature of skincare), the current presentation of the deal requires far more granular financial data to justify the $2 million asking price.

## Key Points
- **The "Triple Engine" Revenue Model:** The business attempts to create predictable, diversified cash flow through three streams: 1) Recurring dues/memberships, 2) A la carte services (facials), and 3) High-margin retail. This structure is positioned as superior to single-service spas.
- **Valuation Critique (Non-Medical Services):** The hosts argue the valuation is "a little rich" because the services are primarily facials and non-medical treatments, lacking the high, reliable recurring revenue stability associated with medical procedures (e.g., Botox).
- **Operational Scalability Concerns:** The labor model is critiqued for being inherently limited by the one-to-one service ratio (e.g., a dentist or massage therapist). This suggests that scaling requires either commanding extreme premium prices or developing a model that increases throughput per employee.
- **Financial Transparency Deficiencies:** The hosts point out that the provided financials are insufficient. They demand that the seller present revenue and EBITDA broken down by store maturity (e.g., most mature store vs. newest store) to account for potential one-time opening costs or ramp-up periods.
- **Margin Confusion:** The hosts flag the conflicting margin data (35% blended margin vs. low 5% net margin), suggesting the seller may be obscuring operational losses or initial investment costs.

## Action Items
- [ ] **Develop a "Store Maturity" Due Diligence Checklist:** When evaluating any multi-location business, demand financial data (Revenue, EBITDA) segmented by the age/maturity of each location to identify one-time setup costs and ramp-up periods.
- [ ] **Analyze Revenue Streams for "Sticky" Recurring Revenue:** Prioritize businesses with multiple, diverse revenue streams (like the "triple engine" model) that rely on memberships or subscriptions, as these provide predictable cash flow and mitigate risk from cyclical service dips.
- [ ] **Model Labor Throughput:** When assessing service-based businesses, calculate the maximum potential revenue per employee per day/week. If the model is strictly one-to-one, determine if the premium pricing is high enough to justify the labor constraint.
- [ ] **Investigate Consultative Sales Models:** Focus on businesses that combine service delivery with high-margin retail/product sales (like skincare) and use proprietary CRM systems to drive repeat purchases and product refills, rather than relying solely on service volume.
- [ ] **Utilize Specialized Acquisition Platforms:** For those serious about buying small businesses, research platforms like CapitalPad to professionalize the capital-raising and deal-sourcing process.
