# Religion is action, not belief — Derek Sivers

## Summary
The episode argues that the true essence of any spiritual or ethical system is not the internal belief structure, but the external, observable actions and organizational practices. The speaker uses the contrast between two men—one driven by emotional belief and the other by disciplined action—to establish that behavior is the primary indicator of character and ethical alignment. The core thesis is that focusing on *what you do* (the practice) is far more valuable and measurable than focusing on *what you believe* (the internal conviction).

The discussion draws a critical distinction between "beliefs" (like ideologies such as capitalism or stoicism) and "religions" (which are defined by structured behaviors, practices, and obligations). Historically, the concept of "religion" itself is a European construct, and the Latin root *religio* emphasizes "obligation." This framework suggests that adopting a system is less about intellectual assent and more about committing to a set of duties and routines.

## Key Points
- **Action Over Belief:** The utility of any belief system is measured by its ability to shape positive actions. If actions are not aligned with stated beliefs, the entire system loses its practical point.
- **The Distinction (Belief vs. Practice):** Ideologies (e.g., Stoicism, Capitalism) are primarily belief systems. Religions, conversely, are defined by their structured behaviors, practices, and organizational commitments.
- **The Power of Obligation:** The Latin root *religio* means "obligation." This suggests that adherence to a system is fundamentally a commitment to duty and routine, not merely a matter of faith.
- **The Relativity of Truth:** Because every religion is defined by its opposition (e.g., Protestant vs. Catholic), no single belief system can claim absolute, universal truth. This realization shifts the focus away from doctrinal conflict and toward shared, actionable practices.
- **Behavioral Alignment:** Focusing on observable practices allows individuals to find common ground and ethical alignment with people from vastly different belief systems, minimizing conflict and maximizing practical utility.

## Action Items
- [ ] **Audit Beliefs for Action Alignment:** Identify 3 core personal or professional beliefs (e.g., "I value efficiency," "I believe in continuous improvement"). Then, list 3 concrete, measurable actions you take daily that *prove* those beliefs are true. If the actions don't match the beliefs, adjust the behavior first.
- [ ] **Adopt an "Obligation" Mindset:** When starting a new habit, project, or relationship, frame the commitment not as a "goal" (a belief) but as an "obligation" (a duty/practice). This shifts focus from internal motivation to external accountability.
- [ ] **Practice Behavioral Detachment:** When engaging in conflict (professional or personal), consciously shift the conversation away from "whose belief is right" and toward "what is the agreed-upon, actionable process."

Religion is action, not belief. One man believed God was on his side. He often lost his temper, hurt people, and did more harm than good. It's easy to see the point of good actions without beliefs. It's easy to see which is better for the world. Someone can practice a religion while questioning its beliefs, or believe its beliefs while not adhering to its practices. So, notice the difference between religion and belief. There was no word for religion in most Asian, American, African, and Australian languages. The idea was introduced by Europeans. Before that, their word for spiritual practices was law, duty, righteousness, or the way. Even the Latin root of religion, religio, means obligation. Following a religion means doing, not just believing. Each religion is defined by its opposition. Protestants are not Catholics. Shias are not Sunnis. Christians, Muslims, and Jews are not Pagans. Every religious believer knows other people believe something else. Therefore, no religion's beliefs are true, since conflicting beliefs exist. Remember, not true does not mean false, but just not the only answer. But we can't say religions are not true, because that would be like saying dinner is not true. It's something you do. It's action and organization. Religion is not just in your mind. People argue that their beliefs are true, and other people's beliefs are false. But if they focus instead on the practices, the actions, they might find they actually have no problem with other people's religion. Beliefs exist to guide your actions. If you're not acting in alignment with your beliefs, you've missed the whole point of beliefs.
