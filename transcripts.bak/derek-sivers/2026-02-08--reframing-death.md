# Reframing death — Derek Sivers

## Summary
Derek Sivers recounts the recent death of his pet mouse, a poignant experience that triggered a reflection on the human tendency to avoid confronting difficult emotions, particularly those associated with loss and mortality. He frames this experience as a catalyst for a broader discussion about the necessity of “re-framing” – specifically, the concept of “heaven” – as a culturally ingrained response to the fear of death. Sivers’ credibility stems from his long-standing work in promoting creativity, self-expression, and unconventional thinking within the tech and entrepreneurial communities, evidenced by his prolific work as an artist and speaker.

The episode centers on a deeply personal observation, using the death of a small creature to illustrate a larger philosophical point. Sivers isn’t offering a theological argument, but rather a psychological one: the creation of comforting narratives around death is a fundamental human drive. He argues that the fear of loss is so profound that it leads to avoidance, a state that ultimately impoverishes experience. 

The core of the discussion is a gentle challenge to the listener to embrace vulnerability and the full spectrum of human emotion, suggesting that actively engaging with sadness, rather than suppressing it, is a key component of a rich and meaningful life. The episode is less a structured argument and more a stream-of-consciousness reflection prompted by a personal event.

## Key Points
- **The Value of Reframing:** Sivers argues that reframing – particularly through concepts like “heaven” – is a deeply ingrained human response to the anxiety surrounding death. It’s a coping mechanism, not necessarily a belief in an afterlife.
- **Avoiding Sadness is Limiting:** The act of avoiding sadness (like avoiding pets or people due to fear of loss) is presented as akin to listening to only major chords in music, missing out on the beauty and depth of the minor chords.
- **Embrace Vulnerability:** The experience of grieving the mouse’s death highlights the importance of allowing oneself to feel the full range of emotions, even painful ones, as a necessary part of a vibrant life.
- **Cultural Conditioning:** The prevalence of reframing concepts like “heaven” suggests a widespread cultural response to death, shaped by a need for comfort and meaning in the face of the unknown.

Re-Framing Death For the last three years, my boy and I have had a pet mouse. We got her from a pet store, and he's carried her in his hand through so many adventures in forests, beaches, and playgrounds. She sat on many little handmade boats down the creeks of New Zealand. Sand castles and Lego houses built just for her. Drawings and stories for and about her. You've never seen a mouse so loved.
 The past six months, she's been next to me on my desk, twelve hours a day, as I wrote this book. Moving slower and wobbling, looking like she's in pain. This week, she kept falling over when trying to eat. Thirty minutes ago, she died. I'm surprised how much I've been crying. As soon as she died, she looked at peace for the first time in months. It led to a thought that seems like a nice end to this book.
 Heaven is such a useful reframing. Maybe it's the original reframing. Death can be terrifying or devastating, so no wonder every culture found a way to reframe it. Some people avoid loving pets or even people because they're scared of the eventual heartbreak and loss. But avoiding sadness is like listening to music with only major chords.
 The minor chords are so beautiful. I'm crying, but isn't that wonderful? It's part of a rich life. And even that is a reframing. It's a useful belief that has helped me love people and pets again and again.
