# Bowling: curve into the target — Derek Sivers

## Summary
The episode uses the physical analogy of bowling and frisbee to explain how to counteract deep-seated cognitive biases and behavioral tendencies. The core thesis is that attempting to force oneself to be "straight" (accurate or true) is often ineffective. Instead, the speaker proposes a compensatory strategy: when a bias pulls you off course, you must intentionally aim in the opposite direction of that bias. This counter-aiming creates a natural curve that pulls you back toward the desired target.

The discussion frames self-improvement not as a matter of pure accuracy, but of strategic counterbalance. The speaker provides several practical examples of common biases—such as externalizing blame, overestimating competence, or underestimating time—and offers the opposite, counter-intuitive belief system as a tool for self-correction.

## Key Points
- **The Compensation Principle:** To correct a bias, do not aim directly at the target. Instead, aim in the opposite direction of the bias; the natural force of the bias will then curve you back toward the center.
- **Bias vs. Truth:** The word "true" means straight and accurate, while "bias" means angled or curved. This linguistic distinction suggests that sometimes, choosing a belief that is *not* strictly true but is useful as a counterbalance is the most effective path to correction.
- **Specific Bias Corrections:**
    *   **Bias:** Blaming others for failures. **Correction:** Assume absolutely everything is your fault.
    *   **Bias:** Underestimating project time. **Correction:** Make your best prediction, then double it.
    *   **Bias:** Assuming you are right/know the answer. **Correction:** Assume you know nothing and have a lot to learn.
    *   **Bias:** Talking more than listening. **Correction:** Assume you know nothing and have a lot to learn.

## Action Items
- [ ] **Practice Counter-Bias Thinking:** When you catch yourself making a biased assumption (e.g., "The market is always X"), immediately identify the opposite assumption and adopt it temporarily (e.g., "I must assume the market is Y").
- [ ] **Time Estimation:** When planning any project, immediately double your initial time estimate to build in a necessary buffer and counteract the tendency to underestimate.
- [ ] **Assume Ignorance:** In professional settings, adopt the mindset that you know nothing and have a lot to learn. This shifts focus from defending your current knowledge to actively listening and absorbing information.
- [ ] **Self-Blame Exercise:** When a failure occurs, resist the urge to immediately blame external factors. Instead, practice the counter-bias of assuming you played a significant role in the outcome.

I stop aiming straight since that's not working. If it always curves to the left, I aim to the right. It feels wrong to aim away from the target, but it curves back to the center. It works. So to compensate, I make my best prediction, then double it. I tend to assume I'm right. Then I noticed I was talking more than listening and I wasn't learning. So to compensate, I assume I know nothing and have a lot to learn. To be clear, the new thought is not meant to be correct. It's a counterbalance to correct for my tendencies. Like aiming the bowling ball or frisbee to the right so that it curves back to the target. Another definition of the word true means straight and accurate. And the word bias means angled or curved. So we can choose beliefs that are not true because they're useful to compensate for our bias.
