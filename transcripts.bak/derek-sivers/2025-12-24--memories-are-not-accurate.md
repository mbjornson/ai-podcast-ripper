# Memories are not accurate — Derek Sivers

## Summary
The episode introduces a powerful cognitive concept: human memory is fundamentally unreliable and prone to systematic error. Using the historical example of students recalling their activities during the Space Shuttle Challenger explosion in 1986, the speaker demonstrates that even when individuals are highly confident (reporting 100% certainty), their recollections often fail to match reality. This insight serves as a critical warning for anyone who relies on personal recall—whether it's remembering meeting details, past performance metrics, or historical events—as the primary source of truth. For those in tech and business, this means that subjective memory must never replace documented data or verifiable evidence when making high-stakes decisions.

## Key Points
- **Memory is not a factual record:** The core takeaway is that memories are reconstructive; they feel like facts but lack objective accuracy. This fallacy of confidence (believing your recollection is correct) can lead to significant errors in professional judgment and decision-making.
- **The Danger of Subjective Recall:** In business contexts, relying on "I remember..." or "We always did it this way..." without documentation introduces massive risk. Past decisions, even if they seemed logical at the time, must be treated as hypotheses requiring external verification (e.g., checking original meeting minutes, project logs, or data dashboards).
- **The Need for External Validation:** The experiment proves that human recall is a weak system. To mitigate this bias, all critical information—especially operational procedures, historical facts, and agreements—must be immediately documented and stored in an accessible, verifiable format (e.g., Notion, Confluence, CRM records).

## Tools & Resources
- **Cognitive Bias Checklists:** Implementing structured checklists for high-stakes decisions to force verification of assumed knowledge.
- **Knowledge Base Systems:** Utilizing tools like Notion or Obsidian to create centralized, single sources of truth that supersede personal memory.

## Quotable
> "Memories feel like facts, but they're not."

## Action Items
- [ ] **Implement a "Documentation First" Rule:** For every meeting, project kickoff, or decision point, immediately assign someone (or yourself) to document the outcomes and decisions *before* leaving the room. Do not rely on post-meeting recall.
- [ ] **Audit Past Decisions:** When reviewing historical projects or processes, do not accept anecdotal evidence. Instead, pull up the original source material (emails, Jira tickets, signed agreements) to verify assumptions about why a decision was made.
- [ ] **Practice "Verbalizing Verification":** Before stating a fact in a meeting ("We launched this last quarter"), follow it immediately with the verifiable source ("...as per the Q3 launch report on file").

## Full Transcript

Memories are not accurate. January 28, 1986, at 11.39 a.m., the space shuttle exploded during liftoff, killing all seven crew members. It was a big deal. As soon as it happened, everyone stopped what they were doing to watch the TV updates. Because it was a school day in America, a psychology professor handed out a questionnaire to his freshman students, asking what they had been doing moments earlier when they had first heard the news. Where were you? Who were you with? What were you doing? Then he saved their answers for three years for the sake of what came next. Three years later, he gave those same students the same questionnaire, asking what they had been doing when the space shuttle exploded. After they answered, he asked how confident they were in their answers. Almost all of them said 100%. Then he showed them their original answers from the day it happened. Nobody remembered correctly. Their answers were all different. Everybody had misremembered their own facts. Memories feel like facts, but they're not. People don't doubt their memory, but you should.
