# Beliefs are not facts — Derek Sivers

## Summary
This episode focuses on a fundamental distinction between objective facts and subjective beliefs, arguing that these two concepts are mutually exclusive. The core thesis is that if something can be proven true with conclusive evidence, it is a fact; conversely, anything requiring the preface "I believe" is by definition a belief—a personal stance on an inconclusive matter.

The discussion uses historical examples, such as Galileo's work, to illustrate how scientific understanding evolves: what starts as a radical belief (lacking proof) eventually becomes accepted objective reality (a fact). The speaker emphasizes that the mere act of declaring "I believe" is a linguistic signal that the statement cannot be universally proven at present.

The overall takeaway is highly meta-cognitive, challenging the listener to analyze not just *what* is being said, but *how* it is framed—specifically whether the speaker is presenting verifiable truth or merely their current perspective on an unresolved issue.

## Key Points
- **Definitional Boundary:** A fact is objective reality verified by conclusive evidence; a belief is a personal stance on something that lacks universal proof.
- **The Function of "I Believe":** Using the phrase "I believe" immediately signals to the listener that the statement is not an established, verifiable truth but rather a subjective perspective.
- **Beliefs are Temporary:** The speaker makes the contrarian claim: "No beliefs are true." This implies that all current beliefs are provisional and subject to change or eventual proof.
- **The Galileo Principle:** Beliefs transition into facts over time. What is considered revolutionary belief in one era (e.g., heliocentrism) becomes established fact in another, demonstrating the fluid nature of "truth" until it is proven.

## Action Items
- [ ] **Improve Communication Clarity:** When presenting an idea in business or tech settings, explicitly categorize the statement: Is this a *proven data point* (fact), or is this our *current hypothesis/stance* (belief)? This improves credibility and manages expectations.
- [ ] **Adopt Provisional Thinking:** Approach established industry norms or "truths" with skepticism, treating them as current beliefs rather than immutable facts. Ask: What evidence would be required to prove this assumption wrong?
- [ ] **Practice Definitional Rigor:** When consuming content (podcasts, articles), mentally flag statements that use strong language ("always," "never," "must") and challenge the speaker on whether they are presenting an absolute fact or a personal conviction.
