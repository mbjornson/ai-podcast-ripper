# Even science isn’t true — Derek Sivers

## Summary
The episode challenges the fundamental assumption that scientific knowledge represents absolute truth. The core discussion revolves around the concept of intellectual humility, arguing that science is not a repository of final answers but rather an ongoing process of refinement. According to the insights shared, every model or conclusion reached in science—from Newton's laws to quantum mechanics—is merely an approximation designed to be "less and less wrong."

The central thesis is that scientific progress involves continually questioning existing findings and superseding them with more accurate theories. This constant cycle demonstrates that there is no single, definitive "truth" accessible through current understanding. Instead, the value lies in the methodology of inquiry itself: being curious rather than seeking to be definitively correct.

## Key Points
- **The Provisional Nature of Knowledge:** Scientific models are inherently temporary; they are not final or complete. Each theory is an invitation for improvement and questioning.
- **Utility Over Accuracy:** The most accurate theory is not always the most useful. Practical applications often rely on simpler, robust "rules of thumb" (like using Newton's laws to launch a satellite) because they are sufficient for the immediate goal, even if more complex theories exist.
- **The Value of Intellectual Humility:** The scientific process emphasizes that questioning existing findings is paramount. This suggests that in any field—business, tech, or personal development—the ability to question foundational assumptions is more valuable than possessing a seemingly correct answer.

## Action Items
- [ ] **Adopt the MVP Mindset:** When solving a problem (business or personal), prioritize finding the simplest, most robust solution that achieves 80% of the desired outcome, rather than spending excessive time pursuing perfect, complex answers.
- [ ] **Challenge Foundational Assumptions:** Regularly ask "Why do we assume this is true?" regarding established processes in your career or industry to identify areas ripe for disruption or improvement.
- [ ] **Embrace Iterative Learning:** View every mistake or failed experiment not as a failure of effort, but as necessary data that refines your model and moves you closer to a better approximation of the truth.

## Full Transcript

Even science isn't true. When I started writing this book, my friend asked me for an example of what I consider to be true. I said, science. He's a scientist, so he got a good long laugh and said, no, no, no. Science is useful, but not true. Then he explained. In the scientific process, nothing is final or complete. No model is true. Each one just aims to be less and less wrong. Every conclusion is an invitation to improve it. Scientists learn about existing findings, question them, and try to supersede them. It's better to be curious than correct. It's intellectual humility. Newton's laws of motion from the 1600s work for most situations. Then Albert Einstein developed the theory of relativity, which showed that Newton's laws aren't sufficient. Then quantum mechanics showed limitations in Einstein's theory. But yet, to land on the moon, or launch a satellite into orbit, we still use Newton's laws, since they're simple and good enough for that purpose. The most accurate theory is not always the most useful, and a rule of thumb can be far from true, but good enough to get you where you need to go.
