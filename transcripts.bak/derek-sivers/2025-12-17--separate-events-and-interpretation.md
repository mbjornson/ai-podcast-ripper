# Separate events and interpretation — Derek Sivers

## Summary
The episode introduces a critical cognitive framework: the necessity of strictly separating objective facts (observable events) from subjective interpretations and feelings. The core premise is that language, whether presented as a suspicious request or a seemingly trustworthy confession, often carries hidden judgments designed to steal clarity if not intercepted at the source.

The discussion uses several analogies—from recognizing manipulative requests at a door to analyzing emotional accusations—to demonstrate how easily we conflate what *is* true with what we *feel* is true. The goal is to adopt an unbiased, detached perspective, much like a police clerk filing an incident report, focusing only on the dry, observable data before addressing the underlying emotions and meanings.

## Key Points
- **The Fact vs. Feeling Distinction:** Feelings are valid and matter for addressing emotional needs, but they must be separated from objective facts. The statement "My mother abandoned me" is not a verifiable fact; it is an expression of a *feeling* (abandonment) based on complex circumstances.
- **The Police Clerk Model:** When processing information or conflict, the primary task is to record only unbiased observable events—the raw data—before attempting any interpretation. This creates necessary cognitive distance.
- **Analyzing Judgment:** When someone makes sweeping negative judgments about another person ("needy, stubborn, inappropriate"), the statement often functions as a projection of the accuser's own unmet expectations, telling more about the speaker than the subject.
- **Changing Meaning Changes Feeling:** While we cannot change what happened (the facts), we have absolute control over the meaning we assign to those events. Changing that meaning fundamentally changes our emotional response and how we feel about the situation.

## Tools & Resources
- **The "Fact Filter" Technique:** A cognitive tool used in communication or conflict resolution where you pause and mentally categorize every statement into two columns: 1) Observable Facts (What happened?) and 2) Interpretation/Feeling (How was it felt?).
- **Police Clerk Mindset:** Adopting a detached, purely observational role when gathering information.

## Quotable
> "We have to distinguish them: get the dry facts with no interpretation."
> "Feelings matter to address them. We have to distinguish them."

## Action Items
- [ ] **Practice Cognitive Pausing:** When receiving emotionally charged statements (accusations, grievances), immediately pause and mentally rephrase the statement by asking: "What is the verifiable fact here?" before addressing the feeling.
- [ ] **Adopt the Observer Role:** In stressful conversations or conflict situations, commit to only listing observable actions ("He raised his voice," "She left the room") rather than interpreting motives ("He was angry," "She was dismissive").
- [ ] **Self-Audit Interpretations:** When you feel a strong negative emotion about an event, write down three things: 1) The objective fact (what happened), 2) Your immediate feeling, and 3) A neutral alternative meaning for the event.
