# Cash Flow 101: Why Profitable Businesses Still Go Broke (and How to Fix It) — Owned and Operated

## Summary
The episode, "Cash Flow 101," addresses the critical financial distinction that profitable businesses can still fail if they do not manage their cash flow effectively. John Wilson, CEO of Wilson (a bootstrapped company that grew from $1M to $40M in revenue), establishes that net profit and EBITDA do not equal actual cash in the bank. The core discussion revolves around the concept that cash flow is the speed and timing of money—how fast you get paid versus how fast you have to pay out. Wilson emphasizes that cash flow is not merely an accounting function; it is the primary determinant of survival and growth, allowing a business to remain operational even if it is temporarily unprofitable.

Wilson stresses that the CEO's most critical job is acting as a capital allocator, constantly deciding where incoming cash will generate the best return—whether that is inventory, marketing, or an acquisition. He provides highly specific, actionable advice on optimizing the cash cycle, such as negotiating payment terms with vendors and accelerating customer payments. The overarching message is that financial mastery is less about achieving high profit margins and more about gaining absolute control over the timing and movement of every dollar.

## Key Points
- **Profit $\neq$ Cash:** The most crucial takeaway is that net profit and EBITDA are poor indicators of liquidity. A business can show a million dollars in profit but have zero cash if that money is tied up in Accounts Receivable (money owed to you) or if the company has significant debt obligations (auto loans, M&A debt) that impact cash but not the profit statement.
- **The Cash Cycle Advantage:** The best-positioned businesses (e.g., top contracting firms) are those that structure their operations to get paid upon customer deposit/completion, while delaying payments to suppliers and payroll (e.g., paying suppliers 30 days after receiving cash). This allows them to self-fund growth.
- **Cost Scrutiny vs. Historical KPIs:** Do not benchmark against outdated industry KPIs (e.g., 50% gross margin). Instead, ask: "Would the customer care about this cost?" If removing a cost (like a fancy office) or adding a cost (like better call center service) does not improve the customer experience, the cost should be eliminated.
- **The CEO as Capital Allocator:** The role of the CEO is to actively manage and allocate incoming cash to the highest return opportunity (marketing, inventory, M&A). Failure to do this leads to stagnation, regardless of profitability.
- **Cash Flow is Control:** The ultimate goal of financial discipline is to move from feeling "hostage to the business" to feeling "in control" of the cash flow, which is the difference between a small and a large, stable enterprise.

## Tools & Resources
- **Cash Out Tracking:** Implementing a weekly "Cash In, Cash Out" tracking system to maintain real-time visibility into liquidity.
- **Separate Capital/Profit Accounts:** Structuring the accounting system to automatically transfer a portion of profit into a dedicated "Capital Account" before spending, treating profit as a controlled resource rather than an abstract number.

## Quotable

>

## Action Items
- [ ] **Implement Weekly Cash Tracking:** Start tracking "Cash In" vs. "Cash Out" weekly to maintain real-time liquidity visibility.
- [ ] **Negotiate Payment Terms:** Aggressively negotiate extended payment terms with all vendors and suppliers to delay outgoing cash.
- [ ] **Accelerate Receivables:** Develop systems to get paid faster from customers (e.g., requiring deposits or payment upon completion).
- [ ] **Audit Overhead Costs:** Review all non-essential overhead spending (e.g., luxury office space) and ask if the customer would notice or care if the cost were removed.
- [ ] **Define Capital Allocation Strategy:** Treat incoming cash as a finite resource and formally decide its allocation (e.g., 60% marketing, 30% inventory, 10% reserve) rather than spending it reactively.
