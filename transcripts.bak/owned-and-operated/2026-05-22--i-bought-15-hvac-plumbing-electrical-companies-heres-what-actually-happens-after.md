# I Bought 15 HVAC, Plumbing & Electrical Companies — Here’s What Actually Happens After Closing — Owned and Operated

## Summary
John Wilson, who runs a home service company and has acquired approximately 15 businesses in the HVAC, Plumbing, and Electrical sectors over the past decade, outlines the full lifecycle of buying a small service business. He emphasizes that the most challenging part of the process is not the acquisition itself, but the operational execution and management required after the deal closes. Wilson frames the entire process as a highly intentional, proactive sales effort, arguing that most prospective buyers fail because they wait for the "perfect" deal to come to them rather than actively hunting for it.

The discussion provides a structured guide covering deal sourcing, valuation mechanics, due diligence, and post-close integration. Wilson stresses that the value of a business is often emotional for the seller (tied to retirement needs) and must be separated from its actual financial worth. He advises buyers to view the acquisition process not as a transaction, but as an engagement leading to a long-term partnership, requiring a focus on fixing problems and building a sustainable, profitable operation.

## Key Points
- **The Core Challenge:** The hardest part of buying a business is running it after you buy it, not the purchase itself.
- **Deal Sourcing Strategy (The "Contact Sport"):** Do not wait for inbound deals. The most effective sourcing methods are highly proactive:
    *   Knocking on doors (physical presence).
    *   Cold calling and letters/postcards.
    *   Joining networking groups.
    *   *Contrarian Take:* The highest response rate comes from two contractors talking to each other, not from formal brokers or capital groups.
- **Valuation Mechanics:**
    *   **General Rule:** Small businesses typically sell for 2x to 6x their profit.
    *   **Industry Specific (HVAC/Plumbing/Electric):** The common benchmark is the last 12 months' profit multiplied by four (4x).
    *   **Critical Distinction:** Buyers must educate sellers that valuation is based on a multiple of **profit**, not **revenue**.
- **Seller Psychology vs. Business Value:** Sellers often state an asking price based on "what they need to retire" (an emotional number), which is frequently much higher than the business's actual, rational market value.
- **Due Diligence Goal:** The goal is not to find a perfect business, but to find a "mess" or an "unoptimized business" that can be fixed. The key question is: *Is the problem fixable, and is it worth fixing?*
- **Timeline:** Once a value is presented and accepted, the buyer should secure a Letter of Intent providing 90 to 120 days for due diligence and exclusivity.

## Tools & Resources
- **Book:** *How to Buy a Small Business* (Recommended resource from HBS for valuation guidance).
- **Process Tool:** The "Process" pamphlet (Wilson's company tool used to educate sellers on the valuation methodology and required cleanup/debt payoff).

- [ ] **Mindset Practice:** Shift focus from finding the "perfect" business to finding a "fixable" business that you will enjoy improving and running.
- [ ] **Networking:** Join industry-specific networking groups to build relationships with other contractors, which is the most reliable source of deal flow.
- [ ] **Financial Education:** Study the difference between revenue multiples and profit multiples when evaluating small business valuations.
