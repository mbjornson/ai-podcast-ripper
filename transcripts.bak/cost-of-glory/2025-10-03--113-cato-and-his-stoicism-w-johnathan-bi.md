# 113 - Cato and his Stoicism: w/ Johnathan Bi — Cost of Glory

## Summary
The episode analyzes Stoicism through the lens of Cato the Younger, arguing that Cato's life and adherence to the philosophy were crucial to its development and understanding. The discussion moves beyond the typical focus on Marcus Aurelius or Seneca, positioning Cato as a foundational figure whose commitment to the philosophy was radical in the late Roman Republic. The core philosophical discussion centers on the nature of suffering: Stoicism posits that suffering arises from "wrong assents"—rational judgments we make about external events.

However, the conversation takes a meta-critical turn, arguing that the modern appeal of Stoicism is less about its philosophical accuracy and more about its function as a powerful coping mechanism. Drawing heavily on Nietzsche, the hosts suggest that people are drawn to Stoicism when they are at the "poles of society"—experiencing extreme loss, wealth, or crisis. The philosophy promises a radical form of mental invincibility, offering a framework for those who feel overwhelmed by fortune, regardless of whether the philosophy itself is the most accurate diagnosis of human suffering (which the hosts compare to the social focus of René Girard or the phenomenological focus of Buddhism).

## Key Points
- **The Theory of Wrong Assent:** Stoicism defines suffering as a failure of rational assent. It is not the external event (e.g., being shoved) that causes pain, but the judgment we make about that event (e.g., "being shoved is bad, and therefore I have a right to feel anger").
- **Stoicism as a Coping Mechanism:** The primary appeal of Stoicism, according to the analysis, is its ability to provide immediate emotional relief. It tells people who are struggling ("down on their luck") that their current suffering—loss of money, abandonment, failure—does not matter for their core happiness.
- **The "Pole" Theory of Resilience:** Stoicism is most appealing to those at the extremes of fortune (the billionaire, the homeless, the emperor). These are the people most profoundly affected by external forces, making the promise of emotional detachment highly attractive.
- **Comparative Suffering Models:**
    *   **Stoic Model:** Suffering is *rational* (a faulty judgment).
    *   **Girardian Model:** Suffering is *social* (rooted in mimesis and metaphysical desire).
    *   **Buddhist Model:** Suffering is *phenomenological* (a practical, experiential training of the mind, exemplified by extreme feats of discipline).
- **The Promise of Invincibility:** The core draw of the philosophy, especially for young men, is the promise of "salvation through learning"—the ability to liberate the mind from false judgments and achieve emotional invincibility.
