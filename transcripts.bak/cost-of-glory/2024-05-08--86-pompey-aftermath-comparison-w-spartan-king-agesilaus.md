# 86 - Pompey: Aftermath, Comparison w/ Spartan King Agesilaus — Cost of Glory

## Summary
The episode uses classical history—specifically comparing Pompey the Great to the Spartan King Agesilaus via Plutarch's writings—to explore themes of lasting glory, political integrity, and the difficulty of maintaining power in a volatile environment. The central thesis is that true enduring legacy (glory) requires not just military success or wealth, but adherence to justice and reputation management, even when facing catastrophic failure.

The discussion frames historical figures as case studies in leadership ethics. Pompey is presented favorably for his ability to rise through chaos while maintaining a degree of honor and integrity, contrasted sharply with Agesilaus, whose methods are depicted as ethically compromised and strategically flawed. The episode also highlights the importance of secondary sources (like Appian) showing that even ignoble deaths can be redeemed by later generations who restore one's memory and dignity.

## Key Points
- **The Paradox of Greatness:** True heroism is rare; its value increases with scarcity. The existence of two equals in a single domain (e.g., Caesar and Pompey) inherently leads to conflict, suggesting that ultimate power often requires the elimination or subordination of rivals.
- **Legacy vs. Power Acquisition:** Plutarch suggests that Pompey achieved prominence through independent service and maintaining his reputation during civil chaos. Conversely, Agesilaus' rise is framed as being built upon questionable actions (sinning against gods and men), implying that ethically dubious beginnings undermine lasting legitimacy.
- **The Importance of Justice in Leadership:** For Pompey to maintain a positive legacy, he was praised for acting with justice—even honoring rivals like Sulla after their deaths. In contrast, Agesilaus is criticized for using political pretexts (like casting out Lysander) that lacked true justification or honor.
- **Reputation Management After Failure:** The story of Pompey's aftermath demonstrates a key principle: even if you die ignobly and your physical monuments are defaced, the memory can be restored by later generations (e.g., Hadrian finding and re-erecting his statues), provided the original death was marked by honor and stoic resignation.
- **Source Depth:** The analysis relies on deep scholarly work, emphasizing that primary sources (Cicero's letters to Atticus, Appian) are often more nuanced and revealing than single biographies.
